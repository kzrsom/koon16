Document guide

Documents need context.

A document can clarify one part of a history while leaving other experiences unrecorded. This route supports careful reading, annotation, and source-status review.

See the annotated model Citation templates
Annotated model

Four questions asked of every paragraph.

Hover or tap a highlighted phrase to see the editorial note attached to it. The model shows how annotations sit beside a text rather than replacing it.

Model document · entirely illustrative text

Administrative description of a district

The officer records the population of the district as composed of several communities1, noting that enumeration was incomplete in the eastern villages2.
Cultivation is described as the principal occupation, with land held according to local custom3.
The report concludes that the district is peaceful and orderly4.

Illustrative model only. No real document, place, person, or figure is reproduced.

Note 1 · Category

“Several communities” is an administrative grouping, not a self-description. The officer’s categories may not match local identity or naming.

Note 2 · Absence

An incomplete count is not a small count. The missing villages should be treated as undocumented rather than empty.

Note 3 · Translation

“Local custom” compresses a set of arrangements into a phrase. What those arrangements were, and for whom, is a research question.

Note 4 · Purpose

“Peaceful and orderly” describes the administrator’s priorities as much as the district. It should not be read as an absence of grievance.

Required fields

Every record needs a traceable condition.

These fields are not metadata decoration. They are the difference between a source and an assertion.

01

Provenance

Repository, collection, creator, date range, custody history, and access conditions.

02

Production context

Why the record was made, for whom, in which language, and under which institutional framework.

03

Corroboration

Which independently situated materials support, complicate, or contradict it.

04

Language notes

Original terms, translation choices, and where meaning may have shifted.

05

Harm review

Whether naming, reproducing, or circulating the record could expose or stigmatise anyone now.

06

Verification status

The present editorial state, with the date it was last reviewed.

Reading procedure

Five passes over a document.

  1. Material

    What is it physically — paper, ink, form, annotations, damage? Material form is evidence of use.

  2. Purpose

    Who made it, for what decision, and who was expected to read it?

  3. Language

    Which terms are institutional, translated, or inherited? Keep original wording beside any paraphrase.

  4. Silence

    What is not recorded, and is that absence meaningful or simply structural?

  5. Circulation

    Who might be affected by republishing it now — and what conditions should apply?

Common errors
  • Treating a count as a population
  • Reading a category as an identity
  • Using a single record as corroboration for itself
  • Quoting a translation as though it were the original
  • Assuming a file’s presence proves an event
  • Assuming a file’s absence disproves one
