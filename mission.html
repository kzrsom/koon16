Our mission

A peaceful platform for dignity and representation.

Our work is grounded in peaceful advocacy, community consent, historical truth, and the belief that every community deserves safety, representation, and the power to shape its own future.

Our commitments Research & evidence
Four pillars

What the mission actually asks for.

The mission is not a single demand. It is a set of connected commitments about voice, land, law, and memory — each of which can be explained, tested, and argued with.

01

Rights and representation

Community voices should be reflected in decisions that affect their lives, land, and future — through institutions that are accessible to ordinary residents.

02

Land and environment

We support the protection of land, water, natural resources, and community livelihoods, with transparent and consent-based decisions about development.

03

Self-rule and justice

We support inclusive governance, accountability, and the rule of law, including the right to question those who hold office.

04

Education and memory

Documentation, education, and dialogue help new generations understand what happened — and what remains disputed.

A lawful civic path

Persuasion, not pressure.

We support democratic participation, constitutional dialogue, accountable administration, and nonviolent civic action. That means working through public argument, lawful institutions, and negotiated agreement — even when the process is slow, uneven, or incomplete.

  • Engage institutions that exist rather than inventing parallel authority
  • Publish reasoning so it can be examined and answered
  • Refuse violence, coercion, and mobilisation by threat
  • Accept that lawful outcomes may differ from preferred outcomes
A shared future

Self-rule as responsibility.

Local self-rule is discussed here as a framework for responsibility, inclusion, and constructive relationships with wider Somali society. It is argued as a route to better services, clearer accountability, and durable peace — not as a rejection of neighbours.

  • Guarantees for minorities and marginalised communities
  • Continued cooperation on trade, water, and mobility
  • Transparent revenue and public spending
  • Negotiated, lawful relationships with federal institutions
Objectives

Six practical objectives, each with a visible test.

Objectives are written so progress can be seen from outside the programme. Where an objective cannot yet be measured, that is stated rather than hidden.

In progress

Explain the proposal

Produce plain-language explanations of what local self-rule would change, in Somali and English, reviewed for accuracy and accessibility.

In progress

Document carefully

Maintain a labelled archive of periods, places, and testimony conditions that other researchers can audit.

Operating

Protect consent

Operate a consent-first route for testimony, including restricted and private holdings with a withdrawal path.

Operating

Open the record

Publish a visible corrections and right-of-reply route, and report how requests were resolved.

Forthcoming

Build local capacity

Create training and paid pathways for community researchers, translators, and reviewers.

Forthcoming

Advance dialogue

Convene lawful, non-partisan discussion between communities, civil society, and institutions.

How to read these labels

“Operating” means the mechanism exists and is used. “In progress” means it exists in partial form. “Forthcoming” means it is designed but not yet available — and should not be described as if it were.

How change is pursued

Five steps, repeated.

Advocacy here is iterative. Each cycle produces a public artefact — a briefing, a page, a record, a correction — so that the work can be followed rather than assumed.

  1. Listen

    Meet residents, elders, women’s groups, youth networks, and local institutions. Record priorities and conditions of participation.

  2. Document

    Gather sources and context. Separate what is documented from what is remembered, interpreted, or alleged.

  3. Explain

    Write the argument plainly, in both working languages, and say what would change our mind.

  4. Engage

    Bring the argument to institutions, media, partners, and critics — calmly, repeatedly, and in public.

  5. Review

    Publish what happened, including objections, corrections, and unresolved questions.

Who this is for

Four audiences, four different needs.

The same proposal has to be legible to a resident, a researcher, an official, and a sceptic. Writing for one of them alone is how civic arguments fail.

01

Residents

Clear information about services, land, representation, and how to raise a concern without risk.

02

Researchers

Frameworks, labels, source conditions, and citation guidance that make limits visible.

03

Institutions

Briefings that state the argument, the evidence, and the specific ask.

04

Diaspora & partners

A way to follow the work, contribute sources, and support documentation responsibly.
