Working notes

Six notes, each naming its own limit.

A working note records what was noticed, what remains unclear, and what would change the note. It is written to be challenged.

Note 006 · Working

Reading a catalogue against local naming

Catalogue entries often use terms that do not match how residents name a place. Recording both — and the difference — is more useful than choosing one. A future note should describe how the comparison was made.

What would change thisEvidence that the catalogue term and local term describe different things

Note 005 · Working

Translation as an editorial decision

A translated phrase can sound more certain than the original. Where a term carries obligation or relationship, the note keeps the original and explains the choice rather than settling it.

What would change thisCommunity preference for a different working term

Note 004 · Open question

Why so few accounts describe ordinary work

Collections tend to preserve conflict and administration. Farming, fishing, weaving, and care appear mainly in the margins. A research route might begin with photographs rather than reports.

What would change thisA collection with substantial everyday material
OwnerLivelihoods research
Note 003 · Review

Consent that changes over time

A contributor may agree to a recording, then ask later that it not be quoted. Treating the later request as final is the only defensible reading of ongoing consent.

What would change thisNothing — this is treated as a settled condition
OwnerSafeguarding
Note 002 · Open question

Maps that show less than they claim

A map’s authority often exceeds its evidence. Scale, date, and the purpose of the mapmaker belong beside the image, not in a caption written later.

Starting the archive with conditions, not content

The first publication decision was to publish labels, access levels, and review steps before any descriptive content. That order protects later work from the pressure of an empty page.

Method in the field

Three things every note must name.

A note that names its own weak point is more useful than a confident summary that hides it.

01

What was observed

Describe the material, the setting, and who was present — without attributing anything that was not agreed to be attributed.

02

What remains unclear

Name the gap precisely. “Unknown” is more useful than a plausible reconstruction.

03

What would change it

State the evidence that would revise the note. This is what makes a note research rather than opinion.

Reading list

Where to start.

A short route for readers new to the material. Nothing here substitutes for source review; each item is a starting condition rather than a settled account.

Begin with the method
The editorial method explains labels, review, consent, and correction. Reading it first makes every other page easier to interpret.
Then read the chronology framework
Periods are research prompts, not events. The chronology shows how the archive organises questions across time.
Then the places docket
Place labels carry conditions. Learning how they are used prevents a common misreading of maps and registers.
Then oral histories
Testimony is the most easily harmed material. Reading the consent conditions first is a form of respect, not paperwork.
Finally the sensitive records notice
Some material should be described only minimally. Understanding why is part of understanding the archive.
